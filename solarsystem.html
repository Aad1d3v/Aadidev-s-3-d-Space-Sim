Getting around

  • Drag anywhere to rotate the view, scroll to zoom in and out.
  • The camera slowly auto-rotates while you're at the overview — grab it any time to take control.
  • Hover a planet to highlight it with a targeting ring; the cursor becomes a pointer.
  • Halley's Comet is in the sim on its real 76-year orbit (last perihelion 1986) — click it in the legend or in space and the camera follows it, with its glowing tail always pointing away from the Sun. Set the date to 1986 and it's diving past the Sun with a long tail; in 2026 it's drifting far out near aphelion.
  • In the 🎛️ controls panel, the 🔆 Brightness slider (40–200%) brightens or dims the whole system — turn it up to spot faint planets against the black, or down for a darker, more realistic space night. It scales the Sun's light and ambient sky together, so planets keep their day/night shading at any setting.
  • Click ✕ on any panel, or press Esc, to back out of whatever you're doing.

Exploring the solar system

  • Click any planet — the camera flies to it and an info panel opens with stats (distance, mass, gravity, day/year length…), fun facts, and a live readout of its true distance from the Sun for the selected date.
  • Every world carries real NASA imagery bundled with the sim — Earth at 4096px with its actual continents, drifting clouds and terrain relief (plus glowing night-side city lights), Jupiter at 3600px, and 2048px surfaces for every other planet, the Moon and the Sun. It all works fully offline.
  • Click the Moon to open its full dossier — what it is, how it was born, a timeline of everything that has ever happened there, and a replayable Apollo 11 landing.
  • Every planet sits on its true orbital path (ellipses, inclinations included — watch Mercury's tilted orbit) with a rotating asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

How to fire a weapon

  • Open 🧨 Sandbox (bottom-left) and pick a weapon.
  • Set its power — the slider (10–100%) or the preset chips. Power scales both the damage and how spectacular the effect is.
  • Click the planet you want to hit — the camera zooms in close and crosshairs appear.
  • Aim at the surface: the crosshair turns red when locked. Click to fire. Meteors streak down from space; nukes mushroom; everything ends in an impact you can't miss.
  • Press Esc to cancel at any step — weapons auto-disarm after firing.

Time & real orbits

  • This is a real orbital simulation: all 8 planets are placed with NASA/JPL Keplerian orbital elements, solving Kepler's equation for the current date. Positions match reality — Earth is ~1.008 AU from the Sun in July, ~0.983 AU in January.
  • Speed slider runs 0–365 days per second (365 = a full year every second). It defaults to 1 d/s ≈ real time — planets creep along their true orbits like real life; raise it to fast-forward through history. 0 pauses.
  • Date picker spans 1800–2050. Jump to any day and the whole system snaps to the true positions — picking a date pauses the sim so it stays there.
  • Now returns to today. 🦅 1969 jumps to July 20, 1969 — the day Apollo 11 landed (the Moon is in its real spot for that date).
  • The Moon orbits Earth on its real 27.3-day cycle with its 5° tilt, so its phase and position are correct for the date too.
  • True axial tilts & spin: every world sits on its real obliquity — Earth 23.4°, Mars 25.2°, Saturn 26.7°, Uranus rolled 97.8° on its side, Venus upside-down at 177.4° — and rotates at its true sidereal rate (Jupiter's 9.9-hour day spins visibly; Venus and Uranus spin retrograde, backwards). Saturn's rings tip with the planet, exactly as seen from Earth.
  • Keplerian asteroid belt: the ~900 rocks each follow their own elliptical orbit with the real period law T ∝ a1.5 — inner rocks lap the outer ones, just like the real belt between Mars and Jupiter.
  • The info panel's Live line shows true heliocentric distance in AU and the planet's real orbital speed from the vis-viva equation — Earth cruises at ~29.8 km/s.

The Moon

Earth's only natural satellite  ·  Born ~4.51 billion years ago

Real surface · 3,474 km across

What it is: a giant rocky world locked in orbit around Earth — the fifth-largest moon in the solar system and the largest relative to its planet. Its gravity raises our ocean tides and gently slows Earth's spin, and it always shows us the same face.

How it was made: about 4.5 billion years ago a Mars-sized world named Theia slammed into the young Earth. The colossal impact vaporized both bodies, and the glowing debris ring clumped together within a century to form the Moon. Molten at first, it cooled into a crust while asteroid storms and volcanic floods carved the cratered face we know today.

Did you know?

  • The Moon drifts 3.8 cm farther from Earth every year — and Earth's spin slows a touch to pay for it.
  • Moonlight takes only about 1.3 seconds to reach us.
  • There's no “dark side” — the far side gets just as much sunlight; it's simply never turned toward us.
  • The Apollo footprints could survive for millions of years — no wind or rain to erase them.
  • The Moon is still alive beneath the surface: moonquakes rumble miles deep, some set off by Earth's gravity squeezing it.

Everything That's Happened There

4.5 billion years of lunar history — from a fiery birth to robots on the far side

~4.5 billion yrs ago

Birth. Proto-Earth is struck by the Mars-sized planet Theia; the debris ring becomes the Moon.

~4.4 billion yrs ago

The global magma ocean cools into a light crust as the first lunar rocks form.

4.1–3.8 billion yrs ago

Late Heavy Bombardment. Storms of asteroids and comets dig out the giant basins we still see.

3.8–3.1 billion yrs ago

Lava floods pour from the interior and fill the basins, painting the dark maria — the “seas”.

~1 billion yrs ago

The last volcanoes go quiet. The Moon becomes the geologically still world we know — and has stayed that way ever since.

Sep 1959

Luna 2 (USSR) becomes the first human object to reach the Moon — an intentional crash landing.

Oct 1959

Luna 3 photographs the far side of the Moon for the first time in history.

1964–65

NASA's Ranger 7, 8 and 9 return close-up photos as they deliberately crash into the surface.

Feb 1966

Luna 9 makes the first soft landing on another world and sends back surface panoramas; Surveyor 1 follows in June.

Apr 1966

Luna 10 becomes the Moon's first artificial satellite.

Dec 1968

Apollo 8: Borman, Lovell and Anders become the first humans to orbit the Moon — and film the famous “Earthrise”.

Jul 20, 1969

⭐ Apollo 11: Armstrong and Aldrin land Eagle on the Sea of Tranquility — the first humans on another world.

1969–72

Apollo 12 and 14–17: six landings, two lunar rovers, and 382 kg of Moon rock carried home. Apollo 13 aborted and returned safely.

Nov 1970

Lunokhod 1 (USSR) becomes the first rover to drive on another world; Luna 16 returns the first robotic samples.

Aug 1976

Luna 24 brings back the last lunar samples of the 20th century. For 18 years, nothing more visits.

1994–98

Clementine and Lunar Prospector map the whole globe and find strong hints of water ice frozen at the poles.

2003–09

A new wave arrives: ESA's SMART-1, Japan's Kaguya, China's Chang'e 1 & 2, and India's Chandrayaan-1 — which confirms water. In 2009 NASA crashes LCROSS into a polar crater and sees the ice directly.

Dec 2013

Chang'e 3 soft-lands with the Yutu rover — China's first touchdown on the Moon.

Jan 2019

Chang'e 4 makes the first-ever landing on the far side of the Moon.

Aug 2023

Chandrayaan-3 (India) lands near the south pole, followed by Japan's SLIM with a pinpoint precision landing.

Today

The Artemis program is preparing to return humans — including the first woman and first person of color — aiming for the lunar south pole.

🚀 Apollo 11 — “The Eagle has landed”

July 20, 1969  ·  Sea of Tranquility  ·  First humans on another world

“That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.”
— Neil Armstrong, 02:56 UTC

On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed the lunar module Eagle on the Sea of Tranquility while Michael Collins orbited above in the command module Columbia. Watch the descent replay, then scroll down for every other mission in lunar history.

  • Broadcast live to an estimated 600 million people worldwide.
  • Armstrong and Aldrin spent 21 hours 36 minutes on the lunar surface.
  • They collected 21.5 kg (47 lbs) of moon rocks to bring home.
  • The guidance computer had only 4 KB of memory — less than a calculator.
  • Alarm lights flashed during descent; mission control cleared the crew to continue.
  • Armstrong took manual control and touched down with seconds of fuel to spare.
  • Six US flags were planted over the missions; UV rays have probably faded them to white.
  • They left a plaque reading: “We came in peace for all mankind.”
